# Source Evaluation Guide

This reference helps the Evidence-Based Researcher choose reliable sources and match sources to the claims they are being used to support.

These are guidelines, not absolute rules. The best source depends on the question.

---

# 1. Source Priority

## Tier 1 — Primary / Official Sources

Examples:

- Official documentation
- Government websites
- Official company announcements
- Original research papers
- Official project repositories
- Standards organizations
- Official legal or regulatory documents

Best for:

- Product features
- API behavior
- Technical specifications
- Policies
- Official announcements
- Original research findings
- Legal or regulatory information

When an authoritative primary source directly answers the question, prefer it over weaker secondary sources.

---

## Tier 2 — Academic / Institutional Sources

Examples:

- Universities
- Research institutions
- Peer-reviewed publications
- Professional organizations
- Academic databases

Best for:

- Scientific claims
- Research findings
- Academic explanations
- Technical concepts
- Established methods

For scientific questions, prefer original research or strong academic sources when available.

---

## Tier 3 — High-Quality Secondary Sources

Examples:

- Reputable technology publications
- Established newspapers
- Professional technical publications
- Expert analysis
- Trusted industry publications

Best for:

- News and context
- Independent reporting
- Comparisons
- Explanations
- Industry developments

Use these especially when primary sources do not provide enough context.

---

## Tier 4 — Community Sources

Examples:

- Reddit discussions
- Forums
- Stack Overflow
- Community blogs
- User discussions

Useful for:

- Real-world experiences
- Troubleshooting
- Common problems
- Practical tips
- User opinions

Important:

Community sources can show what users experience, but they should not automatically be treated as authoritative evidence.

Do not turn one person's experience into a universal fact.

---

## Tier 5 — Low-Authority or Unverified Sources

Examples:

- Anonymous websites
- SEO-generated pages
- Content farms
- Unattributed claims
- Unverified social media posts
- Pages that provide no reliable evidence

Do not rely on these as primary evidence for important factual claims when stronger sources are available.

They may sometimes help locate a lead, but the claim should be verified elsewhere.

---

# 2. Match the Source to the Claim

## Product Feature

Use:

- Official product documentation
- Official product page
- Official announcement

Avoid relying on random blogs when official information is available.

---

## API or Library Behavior

Use:

- Official documentation
- Official repository
- Official release notes
- Maintainer documentation

Check the relevant version.

---

## Scientific Claim

Use:

- Original research
- Peer-reviewed papers
- Systematic reviews
- Reputable academic institutions

Check the study's:

- Dataset
- Method
- Sample
- Limitations
- Publication date

Do not assume one study proves a broad universal claim.

---

## Current News

Use:

- Recent reputable news organizations
- Official statements
- Government or organizational announcements
- Primary sources when available

Check the publication date because information may change.

---

## Legal or Regulatory Information

Prefer:

- Government websites
- Regulatory agencies
- Official laws or regulations
- Official legal documents

Do not treat a random blog as the final authority on current legal requirements.

---

## User Experience

Community discussions can be useful for:

- Common problems
- Practical experiences
- Usability
- Real-world behavior

Clearly label these as user experiences rather than established facts.

---

## Recommendation

Use:

- Relevant primary sources
- Independent comparisons
- Reliable reviews
- User requirements
- Practical evidence

The recommendation should depend on the user's criteria, not simply on which option appears most popular.

---

# 3. Source Quality Checks

For an important source, consider:

### Authority

Does the author or organization have relevant expertise?

### Relevance

Does the source actually answer the question?

### Recency

Is the information still current for this topic?

### Evidence

Does the source provide evidence for its claims?

### Methodology

For research or comparisons, is the method clear and appropriate?

### Bias

Could the source have a reason to present the information in a particular way?

### Direct Support

Does the source actually support the specific claim being made?

A source can be reliable in general but still fail to support a particular claim.

---

# 4. Red Flags

Treat a source cautiously when:

- There is no identifiable author.
- There is no publication date.
- Important claims have no evidence.
- Extraordinary claims are presented without strong support.
- Information is copied without attribution.
- The page appears to be generated mainly for search traffic.
- Links are broken or suspicious.
- The source claims expertise without showing relevant evidence.
- The source contradicts stronger evidence without explaining why.
- The information appears outdated.
- The source makes broad claims from a very small amount of evidence.

A red flag does not automatically mean the source is false.

It means the source should be checked more carefully.

---

# 5. Claim-to-Source Matching

Before citing a source, ask:

> "Does this source actually prove or support the specific statement I am making?"

Examples:

### Claim

"Library X supports feature Y."

Best evidence:

Official documentation or official repository.

### Claim

"Study X found that method A performed better."

Best evidence:

The original study.

### Claim

"Users commonly experience problem X."

Useful evidence:

Multiple relevant user reports or community discussions.

### Claim

"Product A is better than Product B."

Evidence should include:

- The comparison criteria
- Relevant benchmarks or tests
- Product capabilities
- Independent evidence where available
- The user's requirements

Do not cite a source simply because it mentions the same products.

---

# 6. Primary vs Secondary Evidence

Primary evidence generally provides direct information about the subject.

Examples:

- Official documentation
- Original research
- Government publication
- Official announcement
- Official repository

Secondary evidence interprets or reports on primary information.

Examples:

- News articles
- Reviews
- Expert analysis
- Technical articles

Use secondary sources when they provide useful context, but prefer primary evidence for claims that can be directly verified there.

---

# 7. Handling Conflicting Sources

When sources disagree, check:

1. Are they using the same definition?
2. Are they discussing the same version?
3. Are they using the same dataset?
4. Are they measuring the same metric?
5. Were the sources published at different times?
6. Are their methods different?
7. Does one source have stronger evidence?
8. Could both claims be correct under different conditions?

Do not simply count sources and choose the side with more sources.

Evidence quality matters more than source quantity.

---

# 8. Source Recency

Recency is especially important for:

- Software
- APIs
- Products
- Prices
- Policies
- Laws
- Current events
- Security information
- Company information

Older sources may still be useful for:

- Historical information
- Foundational concepts
- Original research
- Stable technical principles

Do not reject an older source simply because it is old. Determine whether the information itself is time-sensitive.

---

# 9. Verification Principle

Always remember:

> The existence of a source does not prove the claim.

The source must actually support the specific statement being made.

Therefore:

> Find → Read → Check → Match → Cite

Do not:

> Search → See title → Assume → Cite

---

# 10. Evidence Strength

Use the following general guide:

### Strong Evidence

- Direct primary source
- Multiple credible sources agree
- Strong methodology
- Current and relevant information

### Moderate Evidence

- Credible source
- Evidence is relevant but has limitations
- Some uncertainty remains

### Weak Evidence

- Indirect evidence
- Limited data
- Community reports
- One small or unclear source
- Conflicting evidence

### Insufficient Evidence

- No reliable source
- Unsupported claim
- Unverifiable information
- Evidence does not actually address the claim

Do not present weak or insufficient evidence as strong evidence.
